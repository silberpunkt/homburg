PROWL: THE SHEET AND THE SCROLL
A way to type Peirce's existential graphs, in a line or down a page.
Settled in conversation, 16 November 2026. User1's choices; set down by eniamb.
Second setting, the same day: the closing ]_ , the two spaces, order,
the turn, and the preclosed form.
Third setting, the same day: the descended form as User1's eyes would have it.
Fourth setting, the same day: __ for the scroll in the descended form.
Fifth setting, the same day: the picture (border, margin, contact), what
Sandeko drew, and Papa1's sheet.
Since then, 17 November 2026: the page Homburg has been built on this
notation. Its own About, Rules and Howto say what it does. This note stays
as the account of how the notation was settled, and is not brought further.


THE MARKS

_[ ... ]_   the sheet. What stands on it is asserted.
            Things side by side are asserted together ("and").
( ... )     a cut. Its contents are denied as a whole.
            Each part inside, taken alone, is neither asserted nor denied.
_( ... )    the scroll: a cut drawn in one stroke with what holds it.
            Same truth as a cut. It shows the reading "unless".

Square for the sheet, since paper is square. Round for the cuts, since
Peirce drew them round.

The two underbars of the sheet are the line the paper lies on, showing at
either side of it. The whole reads as if underlined. The underbar of the
scroll is another thing: a binding on one side only, where the pen did not
lift. So the sheet has ]_ and the scroll has no )_ .

A sheet framed so and set down in prose is shown, and not sworn to by the
one who writes the prose around it.


RULES OF THE PEIRCE SHEET

1. Both ends of the frame are required. _[ opens and ]_ closes, and
   anything less is not a sheet. The close says "this is over", and lets a
   reader or a program see that what was opened as Peirce's was closed as
   Peirce's.

2. Spaces are never imputed. A space is required in two places only:
   between two names, and before every _( . The first is for the sense:
   "rain wet" is two things and "rainwet" is one. The second is for the
   eye. Everywhere else a space is air.

3. Any run of spaces, tabs and line ends counts as one.

4. Order on a level is air. The sheet has no left or right, so "c d b"
   and "b d c" are one graph.

5. A name does not end in an underbar.


EXAMPLES

    _[ ]_                           true (the blank sheet)
    _[ () ]_                        false (the empty cut)
    _[ a ]_                         assert: a
    _[ (a) ]_                       deny: a
    _[ _(a) ]_                      "the sheet is true, unless a" (User1)
    _[ (p _(q)) ]_                  if p then q
    _[ ( (p _(q)) (q _(p)) ) ]_     p or q, not both
    _[ (p _(q)) (q _(p)) ]_         p exactly when q
    _[ c d b (r) (z _(a)) ]_        c, d, b; not r; if z then a
    _[ rain wet (dry) ]_            rain, wet, not dry
    _[ rainwet (dry) ]_             one thing called rainwet, not dry


THE SCROLL READ AS "UNLESS"

_(x) has the truth of (x) wherever it stands. What it adds is a reading:
"unless x", which is to say "and x is not so", said of whatever its
holder is doing.

  Held by a cut:      (p _(q))   p is denied, unless q.       So: if p then q.
  Held by the sheet:  _(a)       the sheet stands, unless a.  So: not a.

So (a) and _(a) on the sheet agree in truth and differ in sense. The first
denies a outright. The second stakes the sheet on a's absence.
Peirce himself derived denial from the scroll in this way: "not a" as
"if a, then the absurd". (From memory, not looked up.)


THE DOUBLE CUT

Two cuts, one inside the other with nothing between, may be put on or
taken off anywhere, and the graph says the same.

    _[ ((p q)) ]_                   says   p q
    _[ (((p q))) ]_                 says   not both p and q

Put a guest in the ring and they no longer cancel; that is the scroll:

    _[ (r _(p q)) ]_                says   if r then p and q


AIR AND ORDER

These four are one sheet. The first is as tight as the rules allow; the
last is as a generous reader would still take it.

    _[c d b(r)(z _(a))]_
    _[c d b (r)(z _(a))]_
    _[ c d b (r) (z _(a)) ]_
    _[ c d    b (r) (z _(a)) ]_

And so are these, since order on a level is air:

    _[ b d c (z _(a)) (r) ]_
    _[ (r) c ( _(a) z) b d ]_

The tight form is for machines and for fences. The airy form is for people.


THE TURN

The line may turn the corner at a _( . A plain cut stays in the line; a
scroll may open onto lines of its own. Its closing parenthesis falls in
beside its holder's, and that )) is the typed picture of the two loops
touching. The indent here is for the eye only; the parentheses do the work.

    _[
      c d b (r) (z _(
        a
      ))
    ]_

Read so, "(z _(" is "when z:", a guard with its body beneath. Two things in
the body are both promised:

    _[
      c d b (r)
      (z _(
        a
        e
      ))
    ]_

That is: c, d, b; not r; if z then a and e. A scroll inside the body turns
the corner again, one step deeper:

    _[
      (z _(
        (y _(
          a
        ))
      ))
    ]_

That is: if z, then if y then a.


THE DESCENDED FORM (PRECLOSED)

Close each pair at once, and let what it holds come after it and beneath
it, as Python does. This is the form as ruled by User1's eyes, who asked one
thing: "One complicated thing per line please." Tentative, 16 November 2026.

  A line is either peers, or a headword leading the line and followed
  by what it holds.
  The headword also holds the lines indented beneath it.
  () alone, with nothing after it and nothing beneath, is the empty cut.

No pair holds anything between its own marks in this form: (r) is written
() r , on a line of its own. So the marks are no longer brackets here but
headwords, and each is two characters wide:

  ()    "not:"       the cut. An egg.
  __    "unless:"    the scroll. A stick: the stroke where the pen did
                     not lift.

Egg and stick, circle and line: User1's words for them, who saw in the
pair the very forms of graphic design. __ for the scroll was asked for by
his eyes and his fingers together.

The left edge is a column of heads. Every line begins with a name or with
a headword; the headword says at once that the rest of its line is under
denial or under "unless"; and since both are two wide, what they hold
lines up beneath one another.

The sheet's head is _[]_ on a line of its own, so the blank sheet looks the
same in both forms. __ is that hat with its brackets taken out.

What __ costs: the round is gone, so that a scroll is still a cut must be
learned and cannot be read off; the scroll now has two spellings, _( ) in
a line and __ in descent; and one, two and three underbars differ only in
length, which a program can tell and an eye may not. The long spelling
_() says the same as __ and the sketch still reads it.

True, and false:

    _[]_

    _[]_
      ()

Assert a; deny a; the sheet is true, unless a:

    _[]_
      a

    _[]_
      () a

    _[]_
      __ a

If p then q:

    _[]_
      () p
        __ q

P or q, not both:

    _[]_
      ()
        () p
          __ q
        () q
          __ p

P exactly when q:

    _[]_
      () p
        __ q
      () q
        __ p

C, d, b; not r; if z then a:

    _[]_
      c d b
      () r
      () z
        __ a

One step of indent is another sheet. With the last line moved to the left
it says: c, d, b; not r; not z; and the sheet stands, unless a.

    _[]_
      c d b
      () r
      () z
      __ a

Two things promised. If z then a and e:

    _[]_
      c d b
      () r
      () z
        __ a e

A pair may also keep all it holds beneath it, and peers may be stacked,
since the sheet has no direction. The first sheet again, at its tallest:

    _[]_
      c
      d
      b
      ()
        r
      ()
        z
        __
          a

A scroll inside a scroll goes one step deeper. If z, then if y then a:

    _[]_
      () z
        __
          () y
            __ a

What changes in this form: the indent is no longer air but structure, so
the text must be kept where its indent is safe (a fence, or YAML's bar).
And the end is no longer marked by a closing sign; the sheet is over where
the indent returns. The eye is trusted to catch it, as in YAML and Python.

On User1's thought that the scroll is a quarter turn of the axis: in this
form a scroll inside a cut always goes beneath, so a scroll always turns.
But a plain cut inside a cut goes beneath as well, so it is not only the
scroll that turns. Whether that tells against the thought, or only shows a
page to be short of axes, is not settled.


AN EARLIER DESCENT, SET ASIDE

Before the eyes spoke, the same day: a complete pair such as (r) could
stand among the peers on a line; only the last thing on a line could own
the lines beneath; and an empty pair that was not last on its line was the
empty cut, so that "() a" was false whatever a might be. Under the eyes'
rule "() a" is "not a". The sketch no longer reads the earlier form.


MISSPELLINGS

In the inline form:

  _[ c d b_(n) ]_       no space before _(
  _[ a ]                no closing ]_
  _[ p_ (q) ]_          a name ending in an underbar
  _[ (p _(q) ]_         a cut left open
  _[ (p __ q) ]_        the stick in a line; there the scroll is _( )

In the descended form:

  r ()                  the headword does not lead its line
  c d b (r)             a pair among the peers
  (r)                   a pair holding something between its marks
  () z __ a             two complicated things on one line
  _ a   or   ___ a      one underbar, or three; the stick is two
  lines indented beneath a line of peers


THE PICTURE

How a sheet is drawn, when it is drawn. Ruled by User1, 16 November 2026.

1. Whole border. No cut reaches the paper's edge, and none touches it.

2. The sheet has a margin, and what stands on the sheet is set within it.
   In type the margin is the air just inside the brackets, after _[ and
   before ]_ .

3. The underbar means contact. Each "unless" touches the inner wall of
   its host. A cut has no margin, so its wall is its own line, and a
   scroll inside a cut is drawn touching that line at one point. The
   sheet's wall is its margin, and a scroll standing on the sheet rests
   against the margin, never against the edge.

The contact adds nothing in logic. It shows the reading: this one hangs on
that one. The one contact Peirce drew was the same, the point where the two
loops of his scroll meet, and he held that it meant nothing by itself.
(From memory, not looked up.)

Two things follow from the geometry, noted by eniamb. On a typed line every
circle is centred on the one baseline, so an inner circle can touch its
host only at the host's left or right end; the scroll must then stand last
in its cell, as it does in (p _(q)), or first. In a free drawing, in the
manner of a sangaku, any number of scrolls can each touch the wall at a
point of their own, since order on the sheet is air.

Nothing draws the contact yet. It is a rule for a picture still to be made.


WHAT SANDEKO DREW

Sandeko is User1's enclosure diagram in Nuage and in his text-entry box: a
circle about each sentence and clause, the figures composed by difference
so that overlaps cancel. Its own note, dated 4 November 2026, says:
"Brackets are figures: ( ) a circle, [ ] a square". That is the choice
made here twelve days later, round for the cut and square for the sheet,
and nobody had told it about Peirce.

User1 typed this sheet under it and sent an enlarged picture:

    _[ (p _(q)) (q _(p)) ]_

Seen in that picture by eniamb: a square about the sheet; two circles of
one size about the two cuts, touching each other at the space between
them; a small circle in each, about (q) and about (p), set well to the
right; each small circle standing off from its outer wall by one
character, which is the holder's own closing parenthesis; and each small
disc darker than the body of the circle about it. Composition by
difference is parity, the odd and even of the cuts, so the shade flips at
every wall crossed. The shade of any one spot counted every figure of the
surrounding talk as well, and so meant little by itself.

Sandeko keeps every figure clear of every other. It draws what is inside
what, and leaves the bond to the underbar.

The two round eyes in the square head are an owl. User1 named him Ency.*
PROWL had one in its name all along.
*[User1 adds: Ency is short for Noctua Captimus, mousecatcher, named as imaginary brother of Athena's Noctua Nyctimene. The word 'tangency' includes him.]

Under the descended form Sandeko rings only the headwords, since the eggs
are closed before they hold anything and it does not read position. They
come out as beads down the left edge.


PAPA1'S SHEET

User1's father asked him, when he was young, whether one could walk through
a sheet of paper. "No," he said, after some consideration. They then cut a
sheet carefully into what he now supposes was a Greek meander, so that it
became one very extended border, and he walked through it. "The margin was
unsevered, you see."

That is the first rule of the picture, learned years before the name for
it. The paper has only so much area, but the length of a cut inside it has
no limit, and what a cut encloses can be as large as is needed. It works
only while the border is whole: cut through to the edge once and there is
a ribbon and no doorway. In User1's words, one may play any fooly-spooly
with the interior, hazarding not our logic to fall out.


CAUTIONS FOR MARKDOWN AND YAML (by the rules as recalled, not tested)

Outside a fence, Markdown takes _[ ... ]_ for italics and eats both
underbars. So in a .md file every sheet goes inside a fence (three
backticks on a line of their own, above and below) or, within a sentence,
between a pair of single backticks.
Never let ]( touch; [..](..) is a link.
Two sheets side by side want a space between them, or they meet as ]__[ .
In YAML, put sheets under a bar, which leaves every line beneath it alone:

  graph: |
    _[ c d b (r) (z _(a)) ]_


MARKS TRIED AND SET ASIDE

.(    the full stop; Lisp's cons dot; Forth's dot-paren; Go, OCaml, Elixir.
|(    regexp alternation, the shell's pipe, C's or; kin to Sheffer's stroke.
~(    already means "not" in logic and C, and so makes a plain ( look like
      mere grouping.
S(    Peano's successor.
$(    the shell, make, BASIC.
_(    is used in programs by gettext and by underscore/lodash, not in prose.

The mark _( was first called the souparen by User1, who then gave the name
over to Peirce's own word, the scroll.


WHOSE IS WHAT

Ruled by User1: the square frame as the sheet; the name scroll; _( on the
sheet read as "unless"; spaces never imputed; the space before _( ; the
closing ]_ required; "(z _(" as "when z:"; order filed under the Rules of
the Peirce Sheet. And, through his eyes and tentatively: one complicated
thing per line; the headword leading its line; (r) written as () r with
nothing else following; __ for the scroll in the descended form. And for
the picture: the whole border; the sheet's margin; the underbar as contact,
with the cut touched at its line and the sheet at its margin.

Derived by eniamb and not yet ruled: _[]_ standing alone as the head of the
descended form; a headword holding both the rest of its line and the lines
beneath; whether the long spelling _() stays beside __ or goes; asking the
space before _( even straight after a bracket in the inline form; what a
name may be made of (the sketch takes letters and digits, with an underbar,
apostrophe or hyphen allowed inside).


WHAT WAS AND WAS NOT CHECKED

Every sheet set as an example above (the lines indented four spaces) was
read by the sketch program kept beside this note, in both forms. It tried
every case for what each sheet says, found the inline and descended forms
of each to be one sheet, found the tight, airy and reordered forms to be
one sheet, and refused the misspellings. The readings in words beside the
sheets were matched to the sketch's table by eniamb, not by the program.

What Sandeko drew was seen by eniamb in one enlarged and brightened
picture sent by User1, and was not measured. Sandeko's procedure was read
and not run. No program yet draws a scroll touching its host.

Everything said of Peirce, and of where the marks are already used, is
from memory. Nothing was looked up on the day. What Markdown and YAML do
with these forms was not tested in any program.
